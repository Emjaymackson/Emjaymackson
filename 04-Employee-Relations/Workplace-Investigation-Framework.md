# Workplace Investigation Framework

## Overview

A workplace investigation is a structured process for establishing facts when an employee-relations matter requires formal review.

The purpose is to determine what happened based on relevant evidence, give appropriate parties an opportunity to respond, and provide management with a sound basis for decision-making.

The HRBP should support an investigation that is:

- Objective
- Evidence-based
- Consistent
- Confidential
- Properly documented
- Proportionate
- Free from unnecessary bias

---

# 1. Investigation Objectives

An investigation should seek to answer:

1. What happened?
2. When did it happen?
3. Who was involved?
4. What evidence exists?
5. Which policies or standards may apply?
6. What explanations have been provided?
7. What facts can reasonably be established?
8. What organizational risks exist?
9. What action may be appropriate?

An investigation should establish facts rather than begin with a predetermined outcome.

---

# 2. When an Investigation May Be Required

Investigation may be appropriate where there are allegations involving:

- Misconduct
- Serious policy breaches
- Workplace harassment
- Bullying
- Fraud or dishonesty
- Conflict
- Unauthorized disclosure of information
- Attendance concerns
- Workplace safety concerns
- Serious performance-related conduct
- Breach of confidentiality
- Abuse of organizational resources

The appropriate response depends on the facts, organizational policy and applicable requirements.

---

# 3. Investigation Principles

## Objectivity

The investigator should approach the matter without assuming that an allegation is true or false.

## Evidence-Based Decision-Making

Conclusions should be based on relevant information gathered during the investigation.

## Procedural Fairness

Relevant parties should have an appropriate opportunity to provide information and respond to concerns.

## Confidentiality

Information should be shared only with people who have a legitimate reason to receive it.

## Timeliness

Investigations should be conducted within a reasonable timeframe.

## Documentation

Important steps, evidence and decisions should be appropriately documented.

---

# 4. Investigation Process

The investigation process can be structured as:

**Intake → Scope → Planning → Evidence Gathering → Interviews → Analysis → Findings → Recommendation → Closure → Follow-Up**

---

# 5. Step 1 — Case Intake

Record:

- Case ID
- Date received
- Source of complaint
- Nature of concern
- People involved
- Department
- Initial risk level
- Immediate concerns
- Relevant policy

The initial complaint should be recorded without unnecessarily altering the employee's account.

---

# 6. Step 2 — Initial Risk Assessment

Assess whether immediate action may be required.

Consider:

- Employee safety
- Threats or violence
- Retaliation risk
- Serious misconduct
- Data or confidentiality risk
- Regulatory exposure
- Business continuity
- Risk of evidence being destroyed
- Risk of interference with witnesses

Where appropriate, the matter should be escalated to authorized leadership, legal or specialist support.

---

# 7. Step 3 — Define the Investigation Scope

The investigation scope should clearly establish:

### Allegation

What is being investigated?

### Time Period

What period is relevant?

### People

Who needs to be interviewed?

### Evidence

What documents or records should be reviewed?

### Policy

What organizational standards may apply?

### Outcome

What questions must the investigation answer?

A clear scope prevents unnecessary expansion of the investigation.

---

# 8. Investigation Plan

A basic investigation plan may include:

| Area | Detail |
|---|---|
| Case ID | Investigation reference |
| Allegation | Issue being reviewed |
| Investigator | Assigned investigator |
| Scope | Matters covered |
| Relevant Policy | Applicable standard |
| Witnesses | People to interview |
| Documents | Evidence to review |
| Target Date | Expected completion |
| Risk Level | Low / Medium / High / Critical |
| Escalation | Required support |

---

# 9. Evidence Gathering

Potential evidence may include:

- Emails
- Letters
- HR records
- Attendance records
- Policies
- Employment documents
- Messages
- Work records
- Meeting notes
- System records
- Access records where lawfully available
- Relevant physical or electronic documents
- Witness accounts

Only relevant information should be collected and retained appropriately.

---

# 10. Evidence Preservation

Where there is a legitimate concern that evidence could be lost or altered, appropriate steps should be taken to preserve relevant records.

This may include:

- Securing relevant documents
- Preserving electronic records
- Restricting unauthorized access
- Recording the source of evidence
- Maintaining appropriate evidence logs

Any evidence-handling procedure should comply with organizational policy and applicable requirements.

---

# 11. Interview Planning

Interviews should be planned around the investigation objectives.

Questions should generally be:

- Clear
- Relevant
- Neutral
- Specific
- Open-ended where appropriate

Avoid questions designed to force a predetermined answer.

---

# 12. Complainant Interview

The complainant should be given an appropriate opportunity to explain:

- What happened
- When it happened
- Where it happened
- Who was involved
- What was said or done
- Who may have witnessed it
- What documents or records exist
- What impact occurred

The investigator should distinguish between what the person personally observed and what they were told by others.

---

# 13. Respondent Interview

The person responding to the allegation should be given an appropriate opportunity to provide their account.

Questions may address:

- Their understanding of the allegation
- Their version of events
- Relevant documents
- Witnesses
- Context
- Reasons for disputed actions
- Any mitigating information

The respondent should not be treated as having committed misconduct merely because an allegation has been made.

---

# 14. Witness Interviews

Witnesses should be asked about matters they personally observed or experienced.

Useful questions include:

- What did you personally observe?
- When did this occur?
- Where were you?
- Who else was present?
- What did you hear?
- What happened immediately before and after?
- Is there any document or record supporting your account?

Avoid encouraging witnesses to speculate.

---
